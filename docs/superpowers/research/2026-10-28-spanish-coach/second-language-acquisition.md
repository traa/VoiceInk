# Spanish Coach — SLA research findings

## Findings

### A. Encounters, incidental vs deliberate learning

**F1. Seeing a word more often helps, but only moderately.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 26 studies, 45 effect sizes, N = 1,918.
- Result: r = .34 between the number of encounters and learning.
- What made the effect stronger: spacing, visual support, engagement, learner age and vocabulary size, and test format.
- Source: Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019), *Language Learning* 69(3):559–599. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12343

**F2. About 10 encounters in context give sizeable gains; full knowledge of a word needs more than 10.**
- Evidence: single controlled study, 121 learners, 1/3/7/10 encounters, 10 different tests.
- Source: Webb (2007), *Applied Linguistics* 28(1):46–65. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/aml048

**F3. Collocations (word combinations) can be picked up incidentally, but need about 15 encounters.**
- Webb, Newton & Chang (2013), single study: 1/5/10/15 encounters; sizeable gains at 15. *Language Learning* 63(1):91–120. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2012.00729.x
- Pellicer-Sánchez (2017), single study: collocations were learned at the same rate as single words; going from 4 to 8 repetitions made no significant difference. *Language Teaching Research* 21(3):381–402. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815618428

**F4. Incidental learning gains are small per word.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis, 24 studies, N = 2,771.
- Share of target words learned: 9–18% immediately, 6–17% at a delay.
- Reading, listening and reading-while-listening gave about 13–17%; watching video gave 7% / 5%.
- Source: Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023), *Language Teaching* 56(2):161–180. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261444822000507

**F5. Deliberate word-focused practice gives much larger gains, which still fade.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis, 22 studies.
- Recall of meaning / recall of form: 60.1% / 58.5% immediately, falling to 39.4% / 25.1% at a delay.
- Activities ranged from 18.4% to 77.0%.
- Source: Webb, Yanagisawa & Uchihara (2020), *Modern Language Journal* 104(4):715–738. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12671

**F6. Producing the Spanish word from an L1 cue builds broader knowledge than recognising it.**
- Evidence: single study.
- Productive learning (L1 → L2) gave larger gains in spelling and in productive meaning, syntax and grammatical use.
- Receptive learning (L2 → L1) won only on recognising meaning.
- Source: Webb (2009), *RELC Journal* 40(3):360–376. https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688209343854

**F7. Retrieval practice (recalling an answer) beats re-studying, and learners can't judge their own knowledge.**
- Evidence: RCT.
- About 80% recall after one week with repeated retrieval, versus about 33–36% when items were dropped from testing.
- Learners' predictions of how well they'd do were uncorrelated with how well they did.
- Source: Karpicke & Roediger (2008), *Science* 319:966–968. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408

**F8. Longer gaps between reviews win on delayed tests; expanding gaps are no better than equal gaps.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis, 48 experiments, N = 3,411.
- Spacing has a medium-to-large effect overall.
- Source: Kim & Webb (2022), *Language Learning* 72(1):269–319. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479

### B. Depth of processing

**F9. How much a task makes you think about a word predicts only a small part of learning, and judging fit is the part that counts.**
- The Involvement Load Hypothesis scores tasks on three components:
  - need: the learner needs the word for the task.
  - search: the learner has to look the word up.
  - evaluation: the learner judges whether the word fits a context.
- Evidence: meta-analysis, 42 studies, N = 4,628.
- It explained 15.0% of the variance in immediate results and 5.1% in delayed results.
- Evaluation mattered most, then need. Search added nothing.
- Sources:
  - Yanagisawa & Webb (2021), *Language Learning* 71(2):487–536. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12444
  - Laufer & Hulstijn (2001), *Applied Linguistics* 22(1):1–26.
- A related checklist, Technique Feature Analysis (Nation & Webb 2011): `[unverified; book not read]`

### C. Glossing (showing a word's meaning)

**F10. Glosses roughly double learning from reading. L1 glosses beat Spanish-language glosses, and multiple-choice glosses work best.**
- Evidence: meta-regression, 42 studies, N = 3,802.
- Glossed vs unglossed: 45.3% vs 26.6% immediately, 33.4% vs 19.8% at a delay.
- Multiple-choice glosses were best. Glosses placed inside the text and end-of-text glossaries were worst.
- L1 beat L2 at every proficiency level. Text, picture and audio glosses did not differ.
- Source: Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020), *SSLA* 42(2):411–438. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263119000688

**F11. L1 glosses help beginners most.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis, 26 studies, N = 2,189.
- L1 beat L2 with g = .33, clearest on immediate vocabulary tests.
- Source: Kim, Lee & Lee (2024), *Language Teaching Research* 28(1):7–28. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168820981394

**F12. Working out a meaning from context retains no better than being told it, and takes longer.**
- Source: Mondria (2003), *SSLA* 25(4):473–499. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263103000202

**F13. Guessing a translation before seeing it has limited, mixed benefits.**
- Potts & Shanks (2014): guessing first helped later memory. *JEP: General* 143(2):644–667. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033194
- Butowska et al. (2021): guessing helped recognition but not recall from the foreign word to its meaning. *Memory & Cognition* 50(5). https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-021-01254-2

### D. Noticing, highlighting, and explicit teaching

**F14. Learners pick up only what they consciously notice in the input (Schmidt's noticing hypothesis).**
- Strength: theory.
- Source: Schmidt (1990), *Applied Linguistics* 11(2):129–158. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/11.2.129

**F15. Visually highlighting grammar forms has a small effect and slightly hurts comprehension.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis, 16 studies. Effect on learning d = .22; effect on understanding the text d = −.26.
- Source: Lee & Huang (2008), *SSLA* 30(3):307–331. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263108080479

**F16. Underlining multiword phrases helps only the underlined phrases; nothing spills over to the rest.**
- Source: Boers et al. (2017), *International Journal of Applied Linguistics* 27(2):448–469. https://doi.org/10.1111/ijal.12141

**F17. Explicit teaching beats implicit teaching.**
- Norris & Ortega (2000), meta-analysis of 49 studies. *Language Learning* 50(3):417–528. https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136
  - Focused instruction overall: d ≈ .96.
  - Explicit d ≈ 1.13 vs implicit d ≈ .54 (figures from secondary sources).
- Spada & Tomita (2010), meta-analysis of 41 studies: explicit beat implicit for both simple and complex grammar, including spontaneous use. *Language Learning* 60(2):263–308. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x

### E. Speaking, interaction, and correction

**F18. Producing language exposes gaps that input alone doesn't (Swain's output hypothesis).**
- Strength: theory plus small studies.
- Sources:
  - Swain (1985), in *Input in SLA*.
  - Swain & Lapkin (1995), *Applied Linguistics* 16(3):371–391. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/16.3.371

**F19. Conversation with feedback supports learning, with large and lasting effects.**
- Theory: Long (1996), in *Handbook of SLA*, pp. 413–468.
- Mackey & Goo (2007), meta-analysis of 28 studies (figures from secondary sources):
  - Vocabulary: d ≈ 1.32 immediately, .96 long-term.
  - Grammar: d ≈ .59 immediately, .99 long-term.
  - Classroom studies .57–.76; lab studies .96–1.20.

**F20. Correction works and its effects last. Prompts (making the learner fix it) beat recasts (just saying it correctly).**
- Li (2010), meta-analysis: medium overall effect that lasts. *Language Learning* 60(2):309–365. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x
- Lyster & Saito (2010), meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies: prompts beat recasts. *SSLA* 32(2):265–302. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990520
- Lyster & Ranta (1997), classroom observation (figures from secondary sources). *SSLA* 19(1):37–66.
  - Recasts were 55% of teachers' feedback, but only 31% of them got any learner response.
  - Elicitation got a learner response 100% of the time.

**F21. Task-based language teaching (TBLT) looks strongly effective, but the effect size is disputed.**
- Bryfonski & McKay (2019), 52 studies: d = .93. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168817744389
- Re-analyses find a smaller effect:
  - Xuan, Cheung & Liu (2025). https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127
  - Boers & Faez (2026). https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688231167573

### F. Chunks (fixed phrases)

**F22. Knowing more fixed phrases makes learners sound more proficient, and pointing phrases out helps.**
- Boers et al. (2006), N = 32: blind judges rated the phrase-noticing group as more proficient. *Language Teaching Research* 10(3):245–261. https://doi.org/10.1191/1362168806lr195oa
- Boers & Lindstromberg (2012), review: learners close the gap on fixed phrases slowly. Helpful approaches are drawing attention to phrases, looking them up, and memorising them. *ARAL* 32:83–110. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0267190512000050
- Theory: Wray (2002); Lewis (1993).

### G. Frequency and coverage

**F23. Vocabulary needed to understand text.**
- To know 98% of the words: about 8–9k word families for reading, 6–7k for listening (Nation 2006). https://doi.org/10.3138/cmlr.63.1.59
- To know 95%: about 4–5k (Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010). https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ887873

**F24. A small core of Spanish covers most speech.**
- Top 1,000 words: 87.8% of spoken Spanish, 76–80% of written.
- Top 3,000 words: 94.0% of spoken Spanish.
- Caveat: the corpus pools Spain and Latin America, so Spain-only forms are under-ranked.
- Sources:
  - Davies (2005). https://mark-davies.org/articles/davies_26.pdf
  - Davies (2006/2018), *A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish*.

### H. Mixed-language ("macaronic" / diglot weave) text

**F25. In mixed sentences, more L1 context means better guesses, and each learner's past history with a word predicts comprehension best.**
- Source: Renduchintala et al. (2016), ACL. https://aclanthology.org/P16-1175

**F26. "Swap in the most frequent content words" is a strong rule that an optimiser using a learner model did not beat; hand-picked guessable words did best.**
- Sources:
  - Labutov & Lipson (2014), ACL. https://aclanthology.org/P14-1053
  - Renduchintala et al. (2019). https://aclanthology.org/D19-1679

**F27. There is no rigorous evidence that diglot weave or Toucan-style word swapping works.**
- What exists: small classroom studies and vendor claims.
  - https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877042814026421
  - https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/hbds/article/view/188983
  - https://www.babbel.com/press/en-us/releases/keep-learning-while-browsing-babbel-to-integrate-toucan-browser-extension
- Origin of the technique: `[unverified]`

**F28. Real bilinguals switch languages only at points where both languages' word order lines up.**
- Source: Poplack (1980), *Linguistics* 18(7–8):581–618. https://doi.org/10.1515/ling.1980.18.7-8.581

### I. Cognates and false friends

**F29. Cognates are easier to learn and less likely to be forgotten, but may be harder to use in context at first.**
- de Groot & Keijzer (2000): cognates and concrete words were learned faster and forgotten less. https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00110
- Rogers, Webb & Nakata (2015), *Language Teaching Research* 19(1):9–27. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168814541752
  - Learners recalled the forms of cognates better.
  - Non-cognates showed larger gains in contextual use immediately after learning.
  - That difference had disappeared one week later.

**F30. English and Spanish share an estimated 10–15k cognates, covering about 75% of the Academic Word List.**
- Sources:
  - Nash (1997), cited in Lubliner & Hiebert (2011). https://textproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/Lubliner-Hiebert.pdf
  - Applied Linguistics (2025). https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amaf041
- Russian–Spanish: no corpus count of cognates found.
- Russian–Spanish false friends, from a practitioner list (not research) at https://taronjaspanish.com/blog/lozhnye-druz-ya-perevodchika-v-ispanskom-25-slov-lovushek-dlya-russkogovoryaschih:
  - *carta* (letter / menu) ≠ карта (map)
  - *firma* (signature) ≠ фирма (company)
  - *arena* (sand) ≠ арена (arena)
  - *conductor* (driver) ≠ кондуктор (ticket inspector)
  - *primo* (cousin) ≠ "first"
- Also likely, not confirmed: фамилия (surname) ≠ *familia* (family); декада (ten days) ≠ *década* (decade). `[unverified]`
- English–Spanish false friends: *embarazada* (pregnant), *constipado* (has a cold), *actual* (current).

### J. Order of grammar development

**F31. Grammar is acquired in a fairly predictable order.**
- Goldschneider & DeKeyser (2001), meta-analysis: how noticeable a form is and how frequent it is explain most of the order. https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9922.00147
- Pienemann (1998), Processability Theory: `[not read]`
- Lichtman & VanPatten (2021): this ordering does not rule out explicit teaching. https://doi.org/10.1111/flan.12552

### K. Personal relevance

**F32. Information related to oneself is remembered better (self-reference effect).**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 129 studies.
- Source: Symons & Johnson (1997), *Psychological Bulletin* 121(3):371–394. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.3.371
- Applying it to Spanish chunks in one's own dictation is my inference; it hasn't been tested.

### What the evidence says about Spanish chunks embedded in your own L1 sentences

No study tests this exact design. Combining the findings:

**What works in its favour**
- The user already knows the meaning and fully understands the surrounding L1 text. That acts like a built-in L1 gloss (F10, F11, F25).
- The content is personal (F32) and is the user's own message, so they "need" it (F9).
- People repeat their own topics, which can supply the 10–15 or more encounters needed (F1–F3).

**What works against it**
- The card is read passively: nothing is recalled and nothing is judged (F7, F9).
- It appears after paste, when the user's attention has moved on (F14).
- Expect incidental learning rates of roughly 10–15% per item (F4), not deliberate-practice rates (F5).
- Highlighting helps only the highlighted items (F15, F16).
- A Spanish chunk dropped where the word order doesn't fit teaches wrong usage (F28).

**How to get the most from it**
- *Which chunks*
  - High-frequency (F24, F26) and recurring in the user's own dictation (F1, F32).
  - Multiword fixed phrases: discourse markers, *tengo que* / *me apetece* / *hace falta*, whole noun phrases (F3, F22).
  - Placed where both languages' word order lines up (F28).
  - Few cognates, with false friends flagged (F29, F30).
- *How many*
  - Keep most of the sentence in L1 (F25).
  - At most one new chunk per card; the other slots go to reviews.
- *When to show the gloss*
  - New chunks: right away (F11, F12).
  - Review chunks: a brief chance to recall before revealing (F7, F9), not a long guessing pause (F13).
  - Keep the word-by-word hover gloss (F14).

## Implications for Spanish Coach

1. **Choose chunks by personal × corpus frequency** (F24, F26, F1, F32).
   - Keep a local index of the n-grams the user dictates most often.
   - Pass the top recurring phrases, together with their Spanish frequency bands, into the LLM prompt.

2. **One new chunk per card; the rest are reviews** (F1–F3, F25).
   - Split `chunkCount` into `newSlots = 1` and review slots.
   - Fill review slots from `reinforcementCandidates` when their source text appears in the dictation.

3. **Encounter targets** (F2, F3).
   - Use `timesSeen` with targets of about 10 for single words and about 15 for multiword chunks.
   - Show progress dots on the card and in My Spanish.

4. **Re-weave mastered phrases at low priority** (F2, F8). *This goes against the current design.*
   - Today the LLM is told to avoid mastered phrases, which throws away free, long-gap reinforcement.

5. **Brief recall chance for reviewed chunks** (F7, F9, F13).
   - For chunks with mastery ≥ 1, show the L1 source underlined and reveal the Spanish on hover or after about 1.5 s.
   - New chunks show the Spanish immediately (F12).

6. **Word-order safety rules in the prompt and gate** (F28, F25). `[INFERENCE]`
   - Swap only whole noun phrases, fixed expressions or discourse markers.
   - Never insert a conjugated Spanish verb where the surrounding word order clashes with it.
   - Reject chunks that cut across a phrase boundary.

7. **Hint first, then reveal, on "close" practice answers** (F20, F18).
   - Give a hint about the rule (e.g. "verb ending, vosotros form") or a prompt to complete, then allow one retry, then show the correct version.
   - Touches `SpanishPracticeGrader` and `SpanishPracticeView`.

8. **Limit self-grading via "Just show me"** (F7).
   - A self-graded "correct" should move mastery up less, or only up to a cap.
   - Nudge the user toward a spoken attempt instead.

9. **Extend review intervals past 16 days** (F8).
   - Add steps such as 30 and 60 days.
   - The shape of the curve doesn't matter much; longer gaps do.

10. **Keep practice in the L1 → spoken Spanish direction** (F6, F7).
    - Add a recognition check (Spanish → meaning) only for items that keep failing.

11. **Optional multiple-choice on hover: "which Spanish fits here?"** (F10, F9).
    - Occasional and opt-in. It partly conflicts with the current glance-only card.

12. **One-line grammar note per chunk** (F17, F31).
    - Have the LLM tag the grammar point (vosotros endings, ser/estar, gustar/apetecer).
    - Introduce points in developmental order: *voy a* before the subjunctive.

13. **Cognate / false-friend tags and gloss language** (F29, F30, F10, F11).
    - At most one cognate per card.
    - Show a "⚠ false friend" warning in the gloss.
    - Write the gloss in the language the user dictated in.

14. **Weekly speaking task built from the user's own topics** (F18, F19, F21).
    - Uses the mic and Spanish speech recognition, with hint-first feedback as in #7.

15. **Short spoken exchange with the LLM after practice** (F19, F20).
    - 2–3 turns that rephrase mistakes correctly and prompt the user to fix them.

16. **Show cards when the user is likely to notice them** (F14). `[INFERENCE]`
    - Avoid high-focus apps such as the IDE and terminal (detected via the frontmost app).
    - Track how often cards get hovered.

**Ideas that go against the current design:** #2 (fewer new chunks than the current growth to 5), #4, #5, #8, #11.

## Myths / weak evidence to avoid

- **"Comprehensible input is enough."** Contradicted by F17, F18 and F20.
- **"Diglot weave / Toucan is proven."** No controlled evidence (F27).
- **"Bolding a word makes you learn it."** d = .22, it slightly hurts comprehension, and it helps only the bolded items (F15, F16).
- **"One memorable exposure is enough."** Words need about 10 encounters and phrases about 15 (F2–F4).
- **"Working out a meaning beats being told."** Retention is the same and being told is faster (F12, F13).
- **"Looking words up deepens learning."** Lookup ("search") added nothing (F9).
- **"Expanding (Leitner-style) intervals are optimal."** Equal intervals work as well (F8).
- **"Self-assessment is reliable."** Learners' predictions were uncorrelated with performance (F7).
- **"Restating the correct form is the best correction."** Only 31% of recasts got a learner response; prompts work better (F20).
- **"The natural order makes grammar teaching pointless."** F17, F31.
- **"Cognates are free wins."** Their forms are easy, but early use in context lags (F29).
- **"More swapped words means faster learning."** F25, F1–F3.
- **"TBLT has d = .93."** That figure is contested (F21).
