# Spanish Coach: research on speaking, listening and pronunciation

## Findings

**F1. Computer-assisted pronunciation training (CAPT) works, but the evidence is weak.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 20 studies, N = 1,014.
- Effect: d = 0.68 overall; larger for beginners and intermediates than for advanced learners.
- The authors say the evidence is "indicative, not conclusive" because the studies are low quality.
- Source: Mahdi & Al Khateeb (2019), *Review of Education* 7(3):733–753. https://doi.org/10.1002/rev3.3165

**F2. Automatic speech recognition (ASR) feedback helps, but only under certain conditions.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 15 studies (38 effect sizes).
- Overall effect: g = 0.69.
- Explicit corrective feedback has a large effect. Simply seeing the ASR transcript has a moderate one.
- Effect is large on individual sounds and small on stress, rhythm and intonation.
- Short use gives no advantage over not using ASR; medium or long use does.
- Practising with peers has a large effect; practising alone has a small one.
- Source: Ngo, Chen & Lai (2024), *ReCALL* 36(1):4–21. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0958344023000113

**F3. Off-the-shelf ASR is not a reliable pronunciation judge for learners of Spain Spanish.**
- Evidence: single study of English-, Russian- and German-speaking learners.
- Whisper-large-v3 word error rate was about 12% for both the English- and Russian-speaking groups.
- Errors cluster on learners' vowels that fall outside the native range, and on voiceless consonants changed by the sounds around them.
- Whisper sometimes "hypercorrects": it fixes the learner's grammar in the transcript (*culturas nuevos* came back as *nuevas*), which hides the error.
- Male voices produced more errors.
- Source: Sarymsakova & Martín-Rodilla (2025), *Phonica* 21:1–23. https://doi.org/10.1344/phonica2025.21.2

**F4. Explicit pronunciation teaching works, mostly in controlled speech.**
- Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015), meta-analysis of 86 reports: d = 0.89 within groups, d = 0.80 between groups. *Applied Linguistics* 36(3):345–366. https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amu040
- Saito & Plonsky (2019), meta-analysis of 77 studies: teaching works best on careful, monitored production of specific features. Effects on spontaneous speech, as judged by listeners, are unclear. *Language Learning* 69(3):652–708. https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12345

**F5. Being understood matters more than sounding native.**
- Evidence: foundational single study.
- A strong accent doesn't necessarily make speech harder to understand. Accent, ease of understanding ("comprehensibility") and how much is understood ("intelligibility") are only partly related.
- The 1999 reprint notes that errors in rhythm and intonation seemed to cost more intelligibility than sound errors.
- Source: Munro & Derwing (1995), *Language Learning* 45(1):73–97. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x
- Reprint: https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.49.s1.8

**F6. Training rhythm and intonation carries over to free speech better than training single sounds.**
- Evidence: quasi-experiment over 12 weeks.
- Both taught groups (single sounds, and rhythm/intonation) improved on read sentences. Only the rhythm and intonation group became easier to understand and more fluent in spontaneous storytelling.
- Source: Derwing, Munro & Wiebe (1998), *Language Learning* 48(3):393–410. https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00047

**F7. Listening training with many different voices improves sound perception.**
- This is "high-variability phonetic training" (HVPT): learners hear the same contrast spoken by many voices and pick which sound they heard, with feedback.
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 79 studies.
- Effect: g = 0.92 from before to after training, and g = 0.67 against control groups.
- Gains last and partly carry over to new words and voices. Training time and number of voices both affect results.
- Sources:
  - Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2025), *SSLA* 47(3):794–827. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263125100879
  - Thomson (2018), review of 32 studies, *JSLP* 4(2). https://doi.org/10.1075/jslp.17038.tho

**F8. Better listening carries over to speaking, but only modestly.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 31 studies.
- Speaking improved 10.5% on trained items and 4.5% on untrained items.
- No strong evidence the speaking gains last or generalize. Individual listening gains barely predicted individual speaking gains.
- Source: Uchihara, Karas & Thomson (2024), *Applied Psycholinguistics* 45(4):591–623. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716424000195

**F9. Early studies: listening-only training improved speaking, and the gains lasted.**
- Japanese learners trained only on hearing English /r/ vs /l/ also pronounced them better, and still did 3 months later.
- Sources:
  - Logan, Lively & Pisoni (1991), *JASA* 89(2).
  - Bradlow et al. (1997), *JASA* 101(4):2299. https://doi.org/10.1121/1.418276
  - Bradlow et al. (1999), *Perception & Psychophysics* 61(5):977. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03206911

**F10. Shadowing mainly improves basic sound recognition, mostly for weaker learners.**
- Shadowing means repeating audio aloud almost simultaneously, a fraction of a second behind.
- Evidence: 43 learners over 9 lessons. Recognition of individual sounds improved in both groups. Listening comprehension improved only in the low-proficiency group.
- Sources:
  - Hamada (2016), *Language Teaching Research* 20(1):35–52. https://doi.org/10.1177/1362168815597504
  - Hamada (2019), *RELC Journal*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0033688218771380
  - Not read: Kadota (2019), a book; a 2025 systematic review (full text blocked).

**F11. Trying to recall a word before hearing it works better than repeating after a speaker.**
- Evidence: single experiment.
- Learners who tried to say the word first, then heard it, understood and produced words better than learners who just repeated. This held immediately and 2 days later, with no loss of pronunciation quality.
- Source: Kang, Gollan & Pashler (2013), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 20(6):1259–1265. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-013-0450-z

**F12. Pimsleur's ever-growing review gaps: what matters long-term is waiting before the first review.**
- Pimsleur's schedule grows each gap 5×: 5 s, 25 s, 2 min 5 s, 10 min 25 s, and so on. The tenth review lands about 113 days out. Pimsleur (1967), *Modern Language Journal* 51(2):73–75. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1967.tb06700.x
- Later testing: growing gaps helped short-term memory, but evenly spaced reviews held up better long-term. Delaying the first review improved long-term memory whatever the spacing after it. Karpicke & Roediger (2007), *JEP:LMC* 33(4):704–719. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.33.4.704

**F13. Saying words aloud improves memory compared with reading silently.**
- This is called the "production effect" and is a robust lab finding.
- Sources:
  - MacLeod et al. (2010), *JEP:LMC* 36(3):671.
  - MacLeod & Bodner (2017), *Current Directions in Psychological Science*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417691356

**F14. Nudging learners to fix their own mistake beats just giving the answer.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies, 827 learners.
- Spoken corrective feedback had lasting effects.
- "Prompts" (hints that push self-correction) beat "recasts" (restating the correct form).
- Source: Lyster & Saito (2010), *SSLA* 32(2):265–302. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263109990520

**F15. Retelling the same story in less time builds fluency, but only if the content stays the same.**
- This is Nation's 4/3/2 technique: the same talk given in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes.
- Learners got faster and hesitated less. Arevart & Nation (1991), *RELC Journal* 22(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/003368829102200106
- In a study of 24 learners, gains lasted only for those who repeated the same topic. de Jong & Perfetti (2011), *Language Learning* 61(2):533–568. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00620.x

**F16. Anxiety is reliably linked to worse results.**
- Evidence: meta-analysis of 97 reports (105 samples, 19,933 learners, 23 countries).
- Correlation between anxiety and achievement: r = −0.36.
- Source: Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky (2019), *SSLA* 41(2):363–387. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263118000311
- Standard anxiety questionnaire (33 items): Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986), *MLJ* 70(2):125–132. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.1986.tb05256.x

**F17. Private speaking practice with ASR lowers speaking anxiety.**
- Evidence: quasi-experiment with 146 learners.
- Learners improved vocabulary, were less anxious about speaking, and enjoyed the language more. This comes from the authors' summary; I didn't see the effect sizes.
- Source: Bashori et al. (2021), *System* 99:102496. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2021.102496

**F18. Computer voices (text-to-speech, TTS) are understood about as well as human ones.**
- Evidence: single study, 29 learners.
- Learners understood TTS and human speech equally well. Only naturalness was rated lower for TTS.
- Source: Bione & Cardoso (2020), *Language Learning & Technology* 24(1):169–186.

**F19. Spanish pronunciation teaching also improves listening. Practice and feedback matter more than phonetics lessons.**
- Kissling (2018), 116 learners: pronunciation teaching made spoken Spanish easier for learners to understand. *MLJ* 102(4):653–675. https://doi.org/10.1111/modl.12512
- Kissling (2013), 95 learners: explicit phonetics lessons and a version with the same practice but no lessons improved pronunciation **equally**. *MLJ* 97(3):720–744. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.12029.x

**F20. Spain-Spanish target sounds.**
- **Tapped vs rolled r (*pero* "but" vs *perro* "dog")**, from Face (2006): http://www.lingref.com/cpp/casp/7/paper1274.pdf

  | Group | Tap correct | Trill correct |
  |---|---|---|
  | 4th-semester learners | 48.5% | 5.1% |
  | Advanced learners | 78.7% | 26.6% |
  | Native speakers | 92% | 86% |

  - Advanced learners mostly say the tap where the trill belongs.
  - Only 4 of 21 advanced learners got the trill right at least half the time.
- **"th" sound (*caza* "hunt" vs *casa* "house")**: learners rarely produce it, even after studying in Spain. Knouse (2012), *Foreign Language Annals* 45(4):512–542. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1944-9720.2013.12003.x
- **Soft b/d/g between vowels**: learned slowly.
- **English speakers** shrink unstressed vowels to "uh"; Spanish keeps every vowel full.
- **Russian speakers** — my inference from comparing the two sound systems; I found no study:
  - They may say unstressed *o* as *a* (*como* sounding like "kama"), soften consonants before e/i, and devoice final consonants.
  - They start with an advantage on the rolled r and the Spanish *j* sound, which Russian has.

**F21. The "listen and repeat" audio-lingual method did worse than a cognitive approach.**
- Evidence: Pennsylvania Project, a large comparison in high-school French and German.
- Learners taught with a cognitive approach did significantly better than audio-lingual learners.
- Source: Smith (1969/1970), ERIC ED038061. https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED038061.pdf

**F22. Michel Thomas: no controlled evaluation exists.**
- There is only a critical review and a supportive book.
- Block (2003), *Language Learning Journal* 27:74–78. https://doi.org/10.1080/09571730385200111

**F23. Thinking in the target language ("inner speech"): descriptive evidence only.**
- Sources:
  - de Guerrero (2005), Springer book. https://doi.org/10.1007/b106255
  - de Guerrero (2018), *Language Teaching* 51(1):1–35.

## Implications for Spanish Coach

Code facts:
- `SpanishMastery.reviewIntervalsDays = [0,1,2,4,8,16]`.
- The grading prompt (`SpanishPrompts.swift:24-27`) tells the LLM to ignore recognition slips and returns `corrected` immediately.
- The 60 s cooldown is in `SpanishCardGate.swift`.
- `SpanishPhrase` is a SwiftData model.
- There is no TTS anywhere yet.

**I1. Make practice "try first, then hear it".** (F11, F13)
- The user speaks first, then hears a Castilian TTS model, then can optionally repeat.
- Replace "Just show me" with "Hear it", available only after an attempt.
- Conflicts with the current design: "Just show me" currently skips retrieval.

**I2. Re-ask within the same session.** (F12)
- Ask the same phrase again a few cards later, or 10–30 minutes later on the next dictation as a fill-in-the-blank card.
- Needs its own timestamp in the card gate.

**I3. Start reviews at 1 day or more.** (F12)
- Change `reviewIntervalsDays[0]` from 0 to at least 1.

**I4. Grade "knew it" and "said it clearly" separately.** (F2–F4)
- Add defaulted fields to `SpanishPhrase` (`pronunciationAttempts`, `pronunciationFlags`).
- The review schedule should depend only on recall.

**I5. Give a hint and one retry before the answer.** (F2, F14, F19)
- Use a single one-line hint, e.g. "the *rr* in *perro* is rolled — try again". This replaces showing `corrected` straight away.

**I6. Make the pronunciation check harder to fool.** (F3, F20)
- Run ASR without a bias prompt.
- Compare the result against a confusion list for the user's native language, led by *perro*→*pero*, plus *z/ce/ci*→*s* and unstressed *o*→*a*.
- Have the LLM judge only the flagged segments.

**I7. Listening drill with several voices, before any speaking practice.** (F7–F9, F19)
- "Which did you hear?" with two options: *pero/perro*, *caro/carro*, *caza/casa*, *cocer/coser*, *término/termino/terminó*, *papa/papá*.
- It never grades the user's speech, so F3 doesn't apply.
- Limit: macOS installs only Mónica (compact) for Spain Spanish by default. Other voices must be downloaded via VoiceOver settings.
  - List the installed es-ES voices and link to the download.
  - Speed and pitch changes are a weak substitute for real voice variety.
  - Native recordings would be best, but that needs a licensing decision.

**I8. Prioritize sounds for being understood.** (F5, F6, F20)
- Order: rolled vs tapped r → full vowels → stress → "th" (opt-in, but always include it in listening) → soft b/d/g.

**I9. Pick chunks based on the user's native language.** (F20)
- Detect the native language from the dictation language.
- Have the chunk prompt favour words containing that language's weak sounds.
- Russian speakers can de-prioritize the rolled r.

**I10. Show stress on the card.** (F2, F6)
- Mark the stressed syllable and play the chunk with TTS on hover.

**I11. Optional "say it aloud" button on the card.** (F13, F16)
- Ungraded.
- Hidden while a call app is in front (`callAppBundleIDs`).
- Add pin/extend so the 8 s card doesn't close mid-speech.

**I12. Optional shadowing session.** (F10, F18)
- TTS reads 3–5 of the user's own sentences translated into Spanish, at 0.8× then 1.0× speed.

**I13. Weekly retell (4/3/2).** (F6, F15)
- Same content each round, at 90 / 60 / 45 seconds.
- Measure words per minute and pauses from ASR word timestamps.

**I14. Low-anxiety design.** (F16, F17)
- Practice is private and short.
- Show progress, not a score for every attempt.
- Offer a whisper mode.
- Only offer practice when the user is alone, using the frontmost app and time of day.

**I15. "Think it in Spanish" nudges.** (F23)
- Lowest priority; don't claim they work.

**Biggest conflict with the current design.** The 8-second passive card is about exposure and reading. Most speaking evidence needs audio, recall and time on task. So the card should stay for exposure plus the say-aloud nudge (I10, I11). Practice and new menu-bar sessions should host the listening drill, try-first practice, shadowing and retell (I1, I7, I12, I13).

## Myths / weak evidence to avoid
- **"Repeat after me" as the core drill.** Recalling first works better (F11), and the audio-lingual method did significantly worse than a cognitive approach (F21).
- **Pimsleur's 5× growing gaps as optimal.** Not better than even spacing in the long run (F12).
- **A native accent as the goal.** Being understood matters more (F5), and even learners in Spain rarely acquire the "th" sound (F20).
- **"If ASR typed it right, you said it right."** ASR hypercorrects, and showing a transcript is the weaker kind of feedback (F2, F3).
- **Listening training alone fixes pronunciation.** The carry-over to speaking is modest (F8).
- **Shadowing improves everything.** The evidence is mainly basic sound recognition in weaker learners (F10).
- **"Explicit phonetics lessons are required."** They added nothing beyond practice and feedback (F19).
- **Michel Thomas is proven.** There is no controlled evaluation (F22).
- **Short ASR practice is enough.** Short use showed no advantage (F2).
- **Prompted inner speech as a learning engine.** The evidence is descriptive only (F23).
- **TTS is unusable.** It is understood as well as human speech; only naturalness is lower (F18).

**Still flagged:**
- Bashori effect sizes (F17).
- Whether Jorge and Marisol are Apple voices (I7).
- Kadota's book and the 2025 shadowing review (F10).
- The predictions for Russian speakers (F20).
